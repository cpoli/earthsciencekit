Seismology examples
===================

One executed example per breakthrough in :doc:`/history/seismology_breakthroughs`,
in chronological order, each with an asserted check of the result it shows.
Download the Python source or notebook from each page. :doc:`/guides/seismology` records units and
limits; all examples use deterministic synthetic inputs and require no downloads.

When the two Lamé constants are equal (a Poisson solid) the speed ratio is exactly sqrt(3) and Poisson&#x27;s ratio is 1/4. With no rigidity the shear wave vanishes, as in a fluid.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Lord Rayleigh found a wave bound to the free surface of an elastic solid, slower than the shear wave and decaying with depth. Its speed solves a cubic that depends only on Poisson&#x27;s ratio: about 0.92 of the shear speed in a Poisson solid. Because it spreads in two dimensions rather than three, it carries the largest motions on distant seismograms.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Augustus Love showed that horizontal shear waves can be trapped in a slow surface layer over a faster half-space, which a homogeneous Earth cannot do. Their speed depends on period: short waves travel at the speed of the layer, long waves at the speed of the half-space. That dispersion measures the thickness of the crust. The layer here is a 35 km crust over mantle.">

.. raw:: html

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Beno Gutenberg placed the core-mantle boundary at a depth of about 2900 km. A slower core bends rays towards the centre, so in the real Earth direct P waves fade near 103 degrees from the source and refracted core waves return only beyond about 143 degrees. In between is a shadow. The ray parameter, which is conserved along a ray, connects the two. This toy Earth has a uniform 13 km/s mantle over an 8 km/s core, so its rays are straight within each shell and its shadow starts later, at about 114 degrees.">
