Volcanology examples
====================

One executed example per breakthrough in :doc:`/history/volcanology_breakthroughs`,
in chronological order, each with an asserted check of the result it shows.
Download the Python source or notebook from each page. :doc:`/guides/volcanology` records units and
limits; all examples use deterministic synthetic inputs and require no downloads.

Each increment is only slightly richer in one component, but because the remainder keeps losing it, the remaining reservoir drifts as the fraction left raised to the power alpha - 1. Taking lead as the end of the uranium decay series, he turned the lead-to-uranium ratio of minerals into ages of hundreds of millions to billions of years, the first radiometric ages.     <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Norman Bowen&#x27;s experiments showed that a basaltic melt crystallizes minerals in a fixed order and that crystals either keep reacting with the melt or are removed from it. Removing them (fractional crystallization) lets the residual melt evolve far towards granite; keeping them in equilibrium limits how far it can go. Trace elements show the difference most clearly: an element the crystals reject is enriched without limit by fractionation, but at most 1 / D by equilibrium crystallization.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="Morton, Taylor and Turner assumed that a turbulent plume draws in surrounding fluid at a speed proportional to its own rising speed. Three flux equations then describe it, and in a stably stratified fluid the plume rises to a height of about 5.0 F(1/4) N(-3/4) set by only its buoyancy flux and the stratification. Following a parcel up the axis shows the rise, the top and the fall back towards the level of neutral buoyancy, where the plume spreads. A vent of finite size tops out a few percent below the point-source height.">

Its height grows as the fourth root of the heat flux, about 8.2 Q(1/4) metres, so a column ten times higher needs ten thousand times the eruption rate. The same height follows from the Morton-Taylor-Turner plume with the buoyancy flux of that heat in the atmosphere.">

Buoyancy drives laminar flow between the walls at a speed that grows as the square of the width, and the magma&#x27;s dissolved water comes out of solution as the pressure falls. Basalt in a dike a metre wide rises at metres per second; a viscous rhyolite barely moves.">
